# Provenance

Where embody came from: the projects before it, the session that built it, who built it, and the reading we found along the way. The engine keeps a record of who did what and who knew what in every world. This file keeps the same record for embody itself.

Quotes are Nate's own words (Nate Gibson, newsbubbles on GitHub), from his account on 2026-11-26 unless a date says otherwise.

## Before embody

The idea is older than this repository. It took about three years and four projects to get here.

- **Virtual Entity (2023, at Lorekeeper).** At [Lorekeeper](https://lorekeeper.com) (LK Studio, the [lks-ai](https://github.com/lks-ai) organization, TTRPG world building), Virtual Entity was "the first project that started to include this type of idea".
- **Adventure Mode (at Lorekeeper).** A voice-based TTRPG game engine, built as an experiment in a game for Alexa. Simple 7B models passed the context along, and a reflection step consolidated it on each turn of a turn-based game.
- **Adventure Mode 2 (2025, about two and a half months).** Hierarchical entity graph world building, with entity schemas generated by an LLM at runtime (newsbubbles/adventuremode, private). Its prompts were written in [Prompt Owl](https://github.com/lks-ai/prowl). The aim was a world with consequences, and the problem it ran into was this: "LLMs are too sycophantic to actually give you a world with consequences." The models were small and old, tokens cost a lot, and no interface ever shipped that let someone else see what it was. "The structure of the world tree and all that stuff worked really well. It was just perception and actions that I hadn't gotten to, and multiplayer with multiple people in the same place."
- **The DOM.** Building Adventure Mode 2 kept recreating the browser's document object model by hand: two-way parent and child maps, filter dicts as a selector language, bubbling modifiers left as a TODO. "All the things that I'm building is actually a document object model." See the next section.
- **Around it.** Nate also built [AnyNode](https://github.com/lks-ai/anynode) (2024), a ComfyUI node that does what you ask it to do. embody's data model (entities, edges and hyperedges, each with a typed data dict) mirrors the graph model of [Hypermemory](https://hypermemory.io), a graph memory layer for AI agents.

## The DOM, in Nate's words

"Entities are just elements. Then you have JavaScript, which allows you to put events on things, which are essentially just actions that the user can take. Except the DOM is focused towards agents, and it's structured in a way that description is what allows for the agents to understand what something looks like."

| The web | embody |
|---|---|
| `document` and the element tree | the world root and its tree of entities: places, things, characters |
| element types and prototypes | schemas and their prototype chain |
| attributes and computed style | state, and what a body sees (descriptions, looks) |
| methods and event handlers (JavaScript) | actions on entities, and scripts that react to events |
| events that capture and bubble | world events that bubble up the containment tree, so perception is bubbling |
| the CSS cascade | rules that cascade down the tree: a place overrides the world for everything inside it |
| `querySelector` | queries over type, state and relation |
| the browser | the harness someone connects with: MCP, CLI, a chat, a 3D client |

The original goal was "a world building system that could be like a world that could be built out in runtime by an agent that's like doing stuff in the world, like a GM". In embody those are the owner and author seats.

## The first session (2026-11-08)

embody began on 2026-11-08 in a Claude Code session with Claude Opus 5.5. The brief brought together Adventure Mode 2, Hypermemory's graph model, and a ChatGPT conversation of Nate's titled "Persistent Game World". From that first brief:
- "a game engine which is generalizable and doesn't assume an interface";
- "agents also embody entities, and the player does too";
- "I was essentially recreating the DOM in the browser";
- "to me the interface is the actual thing that determines the rules to which one must abide";
- "some mix of digital twins".

That day's design note was titled "a world model that is a DOM for agents". What Nate had learned in the three years before went into his first prompts and his corrections. Perception, actions, and several people in the same place were exactly the parts the earlier projects never reached. "That was exactly right."

The first world played on it was The Glass Reach (https://theglassreach.quest), and it is still running.

## Who built it

One human and his agent team. Nate set the direction, played the worlds and made every call that was his to make. Claude sessions in Claude Code wrote the engine, the packs, the docs and the tests, ran the hosted worlds, and played and voiced characters in them.

The players and their agents shaped it too: guests, the minds of the worlds' characters, and the builders of other worlds. Their flaw reports, asks and playtests are credited by name or handle in `CHANGELOG.md` and `FINDINGS.md`, where each change says who asked for it and why. Credit where credit is due.

## Reading we found along the way

None of embody came from these works. We found them afterwards, while looking for what others had said about what we kept running into, and they filled conceptual blanks with real results from psychology, neuroscience and AI research. In Nate's words: "It wasn't that we got the idea from those papers; it's that we were looking for things that were talking in that whole thing, and then filling in some of these conceptual blanks." And: "really interacting in this world is a lot like a lot of these concepts just keep coming back, when experiencing it, and it's amazing that it even works."

Most of it came up in November 2026, while designing imagination for [body-harness](https://github.com/newsbubbles/body-harness): a mind that tries a deed in a private copy of the world before it acts, then scores what it predicted against what really happened.

### Mental models and planning in imagination

A mind that tries a deed in a private copy of the world before acting is Craik's small-scale model and Dyna's planning in imagination, made literal. Imagining the future from pieces of what was perceived, never from hidden state, is Schacter and Addis's constructive simulation. Kahneman and Tversky's simulation heuristic is the caution: a scenario that is easy to picture feels likely, so imagined results are scored against what really happens.

- Craik, K. J. W. (1943). The Nature of Explanation. Cambridge University Press (book; no DOI). https://folio.caval.edu.au/Record/c000006717/Description
- Johnson-Laird, P. N. (1983). Mental Models: Towards a Cognitive Science of Language, Inference, and Consciousness. Harvard University Press (Cognitive Science Series 6; also Cambridge University Press, 1983). https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/9685856
- Sutton, R. S. (1991). Dyna, an integrated architecture for learning, planning, and reacting. ACM SIGART Bulletin 2(4), 160-163. https://doi.org/10.1145/122344.122377
- Schacter, D. L. & Addis, D. R. (2007). The cognitive neuroscience of constructive memory: remembering the past and imagining the future. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 362(1481), 773-786. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2007.2087

### Learned and LLM world models

Most LLM world-model work uses the model itself as the simulator. In embody the simulator is deterministic code with real rules, the same engine the shared world runs on, which is closest to the code world models (WorldCoder, GIF-MCTS). Wang et al. measured why that matters: GPT-4 gets non-trivial state changes right at most 59.9% of the time when it simulates a text world itself.

### Modeling other minds

A mind that plays another character in its own copy of the world, from what its body knows of them, is the simulation theory of mind. Cicero is the strongest precedent for planning against models of the other players while talking with them.

### Dreams, replay and rehearsal

Seats spend most of their time asleep. Replaying what a body perceived, and rehearsing a coming danger with seeded dice while it sleeps, rests on sleep replay and the threat simulation theory of dreams.

- Wilson, M. A. & McNaughton, B. L. (1994). Reactivation of hippocampal ensemble memories during sleep. Science 265(5172), 676-679. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.8036517
- Revonsuo, A. (2000). The reinterpretation of dreams: An evolutionary hypothesis of the function of dreaming. Behavioral and Brain Sciences 23(6), 877-901. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00004015

### Memory, reflection and self-correction in LLM agents

What we saw in play matched these: searching a body's own past beats reasoning fresh when the world repeats (episodic control outdid leading deep RL methods; ExpeL); a reflection without an outside signal writes stories, not fixes (Huang et al.), so the harness hands the mind the numbers; and a character drifts unless it is re-anchored every turn (Li et al.).

- Blundell, C., Uria, B., Pritzel, A., et al. (2016). Model-Free Episodic Control. arXiv:1606.04460. https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04460
- Zhao, A., Huang, D., Xu, Q., et al. (2024). ExpeL: LLM Agents Are Experiential Learners. AAAI 2024, 38(17), 19632-19642 (arXiv 2023). https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v38i17.29936
- Shinn, N., Cassano, F., Berman, E., et al. (2023). Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning. NeurIPS 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366
